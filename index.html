How to read this report

Method & confidence scores

Drug data in Punjab is contested — politicians have quoted 0.06% addiction while the same study they quoted says ~4.5% of adults are opioid users. This report therefore grades every figure.

HIGH — official statistics: NCRB / MHA Parliament answers / court convictions / audited surveys (PODS, PGIMER) MEDIUM — credible research or press reporting that cites official records directly (names, FIRs, seizures) LOW — estimates, extrapolations, or unproven allegations. Presented only as such. GAP — where no reliable data exists (this is itself a finding)
Three structural warnings before you read the numbers

1. Arrests ≠ control. Arrest figures under NDPS were inflated after 2012 and then deliberately brought down — The Wire documents the state government issuing informal instructions both to arrest en masse and then to reduce arrests to make statistics look good. Arrests track political pressure, not the epidemic.

2. Deaths are a floor, not a count. NCRB only records certified overdose deaths. In rural Punjab, overdose deaths are routinely registered as suicide or "accident" — families hide the cause. The real number is almost certainly several multiples higher. NCRB itself admits it "is not maintained" district-wise.

3. Seizures are the tip. Punjab's 2023 seizure of ~47,600 kg of narcotics still left 11,589 cases being registered. A police officer quoted in The Guardian-era reporting: seizures "represent just a small proportion of the drugs making it through and being sold profitably."

What "confidence" is applied to

  • Case / seizure / death counts: HIGH when from NCRB or MHA's written answers in Parliament (auditable, signed); MEDIUM when via a newspaper's transcription of them; LOW when a politician's press-conference figure (e.g. AAP's "68,545 cases since March 2025").
  • Prevalence (how many addicts): HIGH for PODS 2015 (peer-reviewed RDS survey, 3,620 respondents, multiplier method); MEDIUM for PGIMER 2022 (15.4% any substance use, cited but full study not public); LOW for political quotes like "70% of youth" (a misquote of a 600-person sample study).
  • Political links: three tiers — convicted (court verdict), charged (chargesheet filed), alleged (named in statements/press, no charge). No one is described as "connected to drugs" unless they sit in one of these tiers.
Question 1 · WHAT

What exactly is the crisis? The numbers, year by year

Punjab is not "a state with a drug problem." It is the single largest consumption market, seizure point and overdose-death site in India. Four independent series tell the same story: cases, seizures, deaths and addicts all moved in lockstep upward from 2010 to the mid-2020s.

Simplified answer — WHAT

Punjab is India's ground zero: 2.3 lakh people opioid-dependent and ~8.6 lakh opioid users as of the 2015 national survey; 9,000–12,000 drug cases a year; ~50,000 kg of narcotics seized annually; the country's highest certified overdose deaths for three straight years (2022–24); and a state that holds a quarter of India's NDPS convicts while 46,000 cases wait in courts. The epidemic is young (18–35), male, and spread across both villages and cities.

The structural who: why it can't be "just criminals"

The kingpin of Punjab's largest busted racket was a police officer. A sitting cabinet minister carries a 40,000-page drug-money chargesheet. A retired ED official publicly joined the SAD-led anti-drug protest movement in 2026. The who is therefore: an international supply chain wearing local police, local politics and local unemployment as its three layers.

Question 3 · WHY

Why did it explode — and why have every government failed?

Two different questions. The epidemic exploded for structural reasons. It persisted because fighting it is politically and financially costly to the people in power. Both are documented.

3.1 Why the demand exploded (structural causes)

Rich state, no jobs MEDIUM

Punjab grew at ~4% (2022) with unemployment ~6.8%. Young people in a cash-rich, aspiration-driven society with shrinking farms and no industry — "frustration, boredom and laziness experienced simultaneously" (The Tribune, 2015). The addicts are 83% employed: drugs replaced jobs, not supplemented poverty.

Porosity by design HIGH

553 km international border, canals and rivers, good roads right to the fence. After the 1990s insurgency ended, a senior police officer says Pakistan "began pumping drugs into Punjab to weaken the border state." Drones now carry the trade over the fence: 10–15 cross daily, only a few intercepted.

Historical acceptance MEDIUM

Opium (afeem) was socially normal in pre-Independence Punjab (Royal Commission on Opium, 1894); desi daru was the default "nasha". A 1979 rural study found widespread opium use even when illegal; a village tracked for 32 years saw drug/tobacco/alcohol use rise 30% → 39% (1979 → 2011). The epidemic grafted onto a pre-existing culture of use.

Price shock & product switch MEDIUM

As cheaper opium/husk supply was disrupted, the market moved up to heroin (chitta) and pharma drugs (pregabalin, ice) — more addictive, deadlier, and more profitable. 90% of dependent injectors in 2015 injected heroin. Overdose deaths (a largely heroin/polydrug phenomenon) only became visible in official counts from 2019 — consistent with the product shift.

Culture & music LOW

Multiple academic and journalistic accounts document Punjabi music glorifying drugs and the "NRI lifestyle"; a study cited in defence journals links this to youth initiation. Direction is well-supported; magnitude is not measurable.

Border-vulnerability of specific districts MEDIUM

Tarn Taran, Ferozepur, Fazilka, Amritsar, Gurdaspur, Pathankot consistently top every metric — geography, not just policy, maps the epidemic.

3.2 Why governments keep failing (10 documented reasons)

  1. The trade funds the politics that must stop it. "A part of what they earn through drugs sales goes into election campaigns… It's a vicious circle" (Al Jazeera, 2014). Every incumbent has an incentive to be loud and slow. MEDIUM (Majithia chargesheet shows the mechanism in one case: ₹35 lakh paid "for elections".)
  2. The police are inside the product. 100+ officers arrested since 2014; the 2013 kingpin was a DSP. Officers who make big seizures get transferred ("harassment for anti-drug work", per a senior officer's public account) — the system punishes enforcement. MEDIUM
  3. Statistics are managed, not measured. The Wire documents the SAD govt ordering mass arrests in 2012-14, then informal instructions to cut arrests; the arrest series (6,125 → 17,001 → sharp drop) tracks orders, not the epidemic. The 2015 PODS report — the first scientific estimate — was never formally published; its author's key finding (16% hard-drug addiction claim in the 2009 affidavit vs 0.84% in PODS) was weaponised both ways. MEDIUM
  4. Treatment is a business, not a service. 117 of 177 private de-addiction centres are run by 10 people; 60%+ of private centres diverted free government BNX medicine to the black market (Vigilance, 2025); one doctor ran 22 centres; over 5 crore BNX tablets went missing in 2019; relapse within six months exceeds 65%; the government's own recovery rate 2017–2023 was 1.4%. MEDIUM
  5. Courts can't keep up. 46,000 cases pending (2023); a trial takes ~7 years; 79 special courts needed. Traffickers are free to work while cases drift. HIGH
  6. The border problem is national, not state. BSF deployment, fences and anti-drone systems are Centre's domain. Punjab's CMs have had to ask Delhi for anti-drone guns and money. Every state government therefore blames the Centre; the Centre blames state politics. HIGH
  7. Election-cycle politics. SAD-BJP (2012): "Punjab is a transit point, not a consumer." Congress (2017): Gutka Sahib oath, 4-week eradication. AAP (2022-): four successive deadlines (6 months; Aug 2024; Dec 2024; Mar 2025) — all missed, each reframed as "battle won, war continues." No party is ever punished for a missed deadline because every party needs the next election. HIGH
  8. The money is hard to trace. Hawala (up to ~30% of India's remittance flow in grey estimates), NRI accounts in Canada/UK/Australia, shell companies (Saraya Industries), sand mining, liquor companies, real estate. ED's own Bhola case took a decade and still relies on asset attachment, not recovery. MEDIUM
  9. Denial is electorally rational. 2016: Deputy CM Sukhbir Badal quoted "0.06% addiction — lowest in the nation" from the very study that found 4.5% of adults are opioid users. Admitting the scale costs votes; under-counting is the default. HIGH (the misquote is documented).
  10. Success is undefinable. No government measures outcome (addicts per 100,000; overdose deaths; first-time use age) — they measure inputs (FIRs, arrests, seizures), which can be faked up or down. Under the AAP the case count fell 27% (12,442 → 9,025) while certified deaths rose (89 → 106) — the input/outcome divergence is the clearest proof of the measurement problem. MEDIUM
Simplified answer — WHY

The epidemic exploded because a cash-rich, job-poor, bordering state with a history of opium culture met the world's biggest heroin supply. Governments fail because the same three institutions that must stop it — police, politicians, courts — are each captured, underfunded, or slow, and because every party benefits electorally from under-selling the problem and over-selling the crackdown. It is not incompetence alone; parts of it are rational behaviour.

Question 4 · POLITICS

Government by government: promises, records, and the documented links

Three governments have held Punjab since the crisis went public: SAD-BJP (2007–2017), Congress (2017–2022), AAP (2022–present). Each one is documented below with what it promised, what the record shows, and the concrete legal links to drugs.

Simplified answer — SPENDING

An addict in Punjab spends ~₹6,830 a month (≈ ₹82,000 a year). The trade consumes ~₹7,500 crore a year in the state. The government's entire central-subsidised treatment budget for Punjab is under ₹3 crore a year. The ratio is not a policy gap — it is the entire explanation for why three governments, three ideologies, and ten years of "wars" have not moved the epidemic.

Data gaps (a finding in itself)

What nobody can tell you — and why that matters

Pre-2019 overdose deaths, district-wise GAP

NCRB explicitly states district-wise death data "is not maintained." Punjab's death time-series starts in 2019. The decade 2010–2018 has no official mortality count — a period that corresponds to the epidemic's steepest climb.

True prevalence since 2015 GAP

PODS (2015) was never formally published, and no state-wide survey has repeated it. The 2022 PGIMER 15.4% figure is cited but the study itself is not public. All current "improvement" claims lack a like-for-like baseline.

Women's data GAP

Nil reporting of women to de-addiction centres (PGIMER review); the only women-only centre (Kapurthala, opened 2017) was discontinued. Women's consumption is effectively unmeasured.

Black-market BNX scale GAP

5 crore+ tablets missing (2019), 60%+ of private centres diverting (2025) — but no complete accounting of substitution-medicine leakage exists, which also breaks any model of opioid consumption.

Drug money, total laundered GAP

ED/PMLA cases give point estimates (₹200 cr attached, ₹736 cr alleged trail) but no agency has quantified Punjab's total narcotics-derived laundered flows. The 2026 "black economy" study is the first academic attempt.

Border interception rate GAP

"10–15 drones per day, a handful intercepted" is an officer's estimate. There is no official interception success rate — a number that would tell us how much of the supply actually stops.

Pattern: every missing number sits exactly where accountability would bite — mortality by district, prevalence over time, the money, the border. The absence of data is not accidental; it is the state's first line of defence.

Synthesis

The six questions, in six lines

WHAT

India's largest drug-consumption market: 2.33 lakh opioid-dependent, 8.6 lakh users, ~50,000 kg seized/yr, the nation's top certified overdose deaths (2022–24), a quarter of India's NDPS convicts and 46,000 pending cases.

WHO

A five-layer chain — Afghan fields, Pakistani corridors, border mules, Punjabi distributors, NRI financiers — with police and politicians inside the middle layers. One kingpin convicted (Bhola), one minister charged (Majithia), 100+ officers arrested.

WHY IT EXPLODED

A rich, job-poor, opium-culture border state met the world's largest heroin supply at the moment its youth lost work, meaning and outlets. Drones then made the border irrelevant.

WHY GOVERNMENTS FAIL

The institutions that must stop it are each compromised: police are inside the product, politicians are funded by it (one proven, one charged), courts take 7 years per trial — and every party wins elections by under-counting the problem and over-counting the crackdown.

WHERE THE MONEY GOES

₹1 lakh/kg at the Afghan field becomes ₹10–50 lakh/kg on the Punjabi street and ~₹5 crore/kg globally. The traced sinks: elections, hawala to Canada/UK/Australia, shell companies, real estate, mines, liquor — and mostly back into the mouths of the state's own youth (₹7,500 cr/yr).

WHAT IT COSTS

₹6,830/month per addict; ₹7,500 crore/yr for the trade; under ₹3 crore/yr for all of Punjab's central-subsidised treatment. A 2,500 : 1 ratio. No government has failed by accident — the budget tells you exactly how much the problem was expected to matter.

What would actually change the numbers

(1) A repeated, published, scientific prevalence survey every 2 years with district-level mortality — making the epidemic measurable and therefore governable. (2) Border technology funded by the Centre at the scale of the threat (anti-drone coverage of 553 km). (3) Asset-based prosecution as the default: attach first, try second — the Bhola and 2024 forfeiture records show it works. (4) Scaling treatment from a 1.4%-recovery pilot to a public health system (OST at every district hospital, BNX supply under electronic monitoring). (5) A fixed, court-supervised fund from drug-money forfeitures into de-addiction — forcing the trade to finance its own cure. None of these requires new politics. All of them require the current political arithmetic to change, which is why none has happened yet.
